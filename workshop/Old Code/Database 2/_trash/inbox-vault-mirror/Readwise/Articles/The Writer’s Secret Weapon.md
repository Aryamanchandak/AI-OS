## Highlights
- Why So Many Writers Are Athletes
  Exercise acts as an extra twist to open the tap of creativity. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgtys7bkyhrta93gj3tgwqe))
- In the years since writing [that book](https://bookshop.org/a/1882/9781643751375), I’ve come to realize that swimming, and exertion more generally, is intimately tied to how I approach the creative act. This makes sense: I’ve reported on exercise and athletes for years, and I’m familiar with the science that examines how movement [changes our brain](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mind-and-mood/exercise-can-boost-your-memory-and-thinking-skills) ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgtzzev5gjs71fxvfh5g8a3))
- Moving while reading, listening, or writing activates a unique mode of learning (a similar distinction can be found in [handwriting versus typing](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/01/110119095458.htm)) ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgwq29az6bz9wh2ty7mbp18))
- And exercise specifically [boosts neurotransmitters](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5928534/) such as dopamine, serotonin, and noradrenaline; it immediately improves our ability to pay attention ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgx8ws9kpbqez0qqrfbkrw7))
- Movement also shakes loose new connections: It’s been associated with both [better creative cognition](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-68632-9) and an increase in the originality and fluency of ideas. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgx945f11fzv38909qqq900))
- In other words, we’re built to move. I started asking other writers I knew whether exercise felt similarly entwined with their work. Recently, the writer and comedian Chris Duffy and I went surfing together, and we chatted in the lulls between swells. “Sitting in the ocean waiting for a wave is the exact same skill as sitting at my computer with the white noise on, waiting for a joke to come,” he said ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgxb547whw0vf3ffvxad6wb))
- Movement keeps us grounded in the sensory details of the landscapes we inhabit, and asks us to pay close attention to what it really *feels* like to do the thing. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgxbnfb6q3gfk8pdw743vnr))
- The writer Ivy Pochoda, who is a former professional squash champion, told me that the euphoria of playing made it into her recent novel, [*Ecstasy*](https://bookshop.org/a/12476/9780593851173), which is about dance and focuses heavily on the idea of stepping outside of yourself. “When you’re in the zone playing, you almost have an out-of-body experience,” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgxcn1whbzcs8jszrr7d4mw))
- The body is an important part of the learning process: Research shows that “moving your foot and understanding the word ‘kick’ are governed by similar areas of the motor cortex,” the cognitive scientist Sian Beilock [writes](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/choke/201207/how-humans-learn-lessons-the-sea-squirt). “It’s hard to separate the reading mind from the doing one.” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgxd1t79v4vbra4dv5kxnyh))
- Joyce Carol Oates once [described](https://archive.nytimes.com/www.nytimes.com/library/books/071999oates-writing.html) running as an act in which “the mind flies with the body; the mysterious efflorescence of language seems to pulse in the brain, in rhythm with our feet and the swinging of our arms.” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgxehe6gtgcshjdptvb9p65))
- Goethe, I’d learned, was one of them; as he got older, he came to dislike sitting at a desk and had to [walk around](https://www.open.ac.uk/blogs/literarytourist/?p=93) while writing. Nietzsche [said the same](https://www.cabinetmagazine.org/issues/32/pendle.php), criticizing Flaubert and the sedentary life—*das sitzfleisch*, literally “sitting meat”—as anathema to creative thought. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgxf22gg8vbb10mqmmhgmay))
- Writers have long been chasing that dopamine hit. Virginia Woolf tramped along the Cornish coast; Oliver Sacks was known to swim; Haruki Murakami is an accomplished runner ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgxgep650j5jw45w2efk4tn))
- Movement loosens something within all of us. Physical practice is not just a break from sitting at a desk—it is explicit and essential, an extra twist to open the tap, allowing for a freer flow from the faucet of thought ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgxgpdqvm9p7h1jzh6wc4jn))
- In “[Street Haunting](https://yalereview.org/article/street-haunting-a-london-adventure?src=longreads),” Woolf’s evocative essay about a wintry walk across London, the restless, lonely writer leaves home, ostensibly to buy a pencil ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgxhx6mksp8v74k174rra3r))
- At the end, Woolf admits that the pencil was an excuse to roam. The walk frees her; it makes her work possible. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgxhtqrc5xf5xaaexkffx61))
- Most days, my own feet are at the edge of the ocean by first light. As dawn reaches its pink fingers across the sky, I paddle my surfboard out into the Pacific. Sometimes, there is nothing but noticing in my head: the light; the seals, bobbing and watchful; the next set out on the horizon. Other times, a problem bubbles up as I arrive—my movements on the wave twitchy and inelegant, mirroring my state of mind—only to recede as my body finds its cadence, in concert with the water. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgxmqa04r705xg3ddzktz5k))
- If I don’t swim, if I don’t surf, if I don’t *flow*, the words won’t, either. There’s a permeability between the practices of writing and moving; one discipline informs the other. In that meeting, everything expands. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01kfgxmfq509ayxgf11v3bkcw2))
