# When a sourcing rule reaches the project ledger

*An illustrative fixture showing how Consequence Radar separates a policy premise, procurement transmission, assumptions, and an Indian-equity operating endpoint.*

> Sample only. Source IDs below refer to the accompanying frozen fixture export, not a live 2026 research run.

## Why this matters

The supplied batch reports that an Indian solar-module sourcing rule is scheduled to take effect. The factual status in this fixture is a formal policy timetable, not a measured outcome. That distinction matters: the analysis can treat the rule and date as reported facts while keeping future procurement behaviour, module pricing, and developer economics conditional. The strongest route into Indian equities is not “policy supports renewable stocks.” It runs through which modules qualify, whether domestic supply can meet near-term demand, the difference between domestic and imported procurement costs, and the share of those costs retained in project returns rather than passed through in tariffs or contract prices. [S1]

## The causal chain

The immediate effect is a change in the eligible module pool for covered projects. If projects that previously expected to use lower-cost imported modules must source from an approved domestic list, procurement choice narrows. That can raise demand for qualifying domestic capacity, but it does not establish that realised selling prices or margins rise. Capacity additions, utilisation, contract structure, technology mix, and customer bargaining power all sit between eligibility and producer EBITDA. The graph therefore records domestic utilisation as an intermediate metric and producer EBITDA as a conditional company or sector endpoint, not as an automatic consequence.

For developers, the same premise points in the opposite operating direction when qualifying domestic modules cost more than the displaced alternative. Modules are one component of total project capital expenditure. Any price gap affects project cost only in proportion to module intensity, and project EBITDA or returns change only to the extent that the developer cannot pass the cost through, renegotiate, delay procurement, redesign the project, or benefit from lower financing and balance-of-system costs. This is why the fixture stores a range calculation rather than a single precise impact: eligible project volume multiplied by module requirement and a sourced module-price range, followed by a sensitivity check on the assumed price gap. [S2]

The Indian-equity destination is consequently two-sided. Qualifying domestic manufacturers may see stronger addressable demand and utilisation, subject to available capacity and competitive pricing. Listed developers may face higher near-term procurement requirements on affected projects, subject to contract pass-through and the timing of tenders. The graph does not collapse these into a generic renewable-sector direction. It keeps the producer-volume channel and developer-project-return channel separate, then allows them to converge only where a diversified listed company has both exposures.

## Central case

The most-supported central case is that the rule shifts a portion of covered demand toward qualifying domestic supply over the next twelve to twenty-four months, improving utilisation before it necessarily improves manufacturer margins. This path is central because the reported rule and eligibility mechanism are directly supported, while the endpoint stops at an operating metric that can be checked in disclosures. It does not require a forecast share price or an invented probability. The corresponding developer implication remains a directional risk to project economics rather than a quantified earnings forecast because the fixture lacks sufficiently comparable, company-specific pass-through coefficients.

Three assumptions carry most of the story. First, implementation occurs on the stated timetable. Second, the covered project pipeline is large enough relative to qualifying supply for the rule to alter procurement rather than merely relabel existing orders. Third, developers retain at least part of any procurement-cost difference. The first is sourced to the policy timetable; the second combines disclosed capacity and pipeline evidence; the third is an inference and is labelled as such. If developers pass through the full cost difference, the developer-EBITDA branch weakens even while the manufacturer-utilisation branch remains intact.

## Alternatives and disproof

A material alternative arises if qualifying capacity expands faster than covered demand. In that branch, eligibility still shifts sourcing, but competition prevents a sustained price premium. Manufacturer volumes can rise without a corresponding margin gain, and developers experience little incremental cost. This is a genuine alternative because it changes both Indian-equity endpoints and follows from a material supply-demand assumption. A second idea—an immediate broad re-rating of renewable shares—is a broken path, not an alternative case, because the available evidence does not supply the earnings and valuation assumptions needed to support it.

The view would weaken most if implementation were delayed, exemptions broadened the eligible import pool, covered tender volume fell, or disclosures showed that qualifying domestic capacity already exceeded demand at competitive prices. It would also weaken if developer contracts demonstrated full and timely pass-through of module-cost changes. Those observations target named mechanisms in the graph. They do not merely say that markets are uncertain.

The result is therefore a testable operating hypothesis. Watch the implemented eligibility schedule, approved capacity, tender volumes, module price gaps, manufacturer utilisation, and project contract terms. Until those observations arrive, the central case is the best-supported coherent route in the supplied fixture—not a guarantee, a trading instruction, or a claim that every company in the sector is affected in the same way.
