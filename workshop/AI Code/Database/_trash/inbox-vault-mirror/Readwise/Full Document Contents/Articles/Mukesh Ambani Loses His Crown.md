##### Ambani’s cup of woes

The war in the Middle East has taken a toll on Mukesh Ambani. His [net worth has shrunk by $16.9bn](https://www.ft.com/content/997c6169-d1c0-42f2-9f09-c6ab4d12ac4a?syn-25a6b1a6=1) this year — the sharpest decline among Asian billionaires — and stands at about $91bn, according to the Bloomberg Billionaires index. Consequently, the other Big A from India, Gautam Adani, has reclaimed his position as the wealthiest person in Asia. India’s richest individuals have collectively lost $28.1bn so far this year, according to Bloomberg, with the geopolitical tensions disrupting fuel prices and availability. Still, with most of his wealth tied to infrastructure assets such as ports and power generation, Adani has been relatively insulated.

For Reliance Industries, however, the pressure that was building even before the Middle East conflict due to [Donald Trump’s ultimatums](https://www.ft.com/content/92e3e6c6-62d2-4c98-8af0-8bac2768592e?syn-25a6b1a6=1) urging India to stop buying Russian oil, is likely to persist. Analysts have lowered revenue and earnings per share forecasts, with the US investment bank Jefferies predicting a 6 per cent decline in the latter for the current financial year. Oil prices have been extremely volatile, moving with each of Trump’s frequent statements on the war, making it difficult even for Asia’s now second-richest man to stay ahead of the turbulence.

While petrochemicals account for more than 50 per cent of the group’s business, there are challenges in other segments too. In January, several brokerages including Macquarie, Citi and UBS cut their target price on the stock, citing [difficult circumstances for the group’s retail ventures](https://www.ft.com/content/0f6f5feb-e113-432e-8190-68e4a05adf6a?syn-25a6b1a6=1). Last quarter’s results showed 8 per cent annual growth in Reliance retail, well below the target of 20 per cent stated by the head of the retail business and Ambani’s daughter, Isha.

Another driver of value for the stock is the [potential listing of the company’s telecom venture](https://www.ft.com/content/1fceb9aa-8f32-4095-a115-aa490898b0e2?syn-25a6b1a6=1), Jio. However, geopolitical uncertainty has damped market sentiment, making it difficult to identify a favourable window for the IPO. The company’s financial services joint venture with BlackRock has also [experienced a muted start](https://www.ft.com/content/b7510eda-20ef-4d94-933f-3c8ea31b041f?syn-25a6b1a6=1), with assets under management falling since last year’s launch.

Reliance’s stock has declined 13 per cent since the beginning of this calendar year. The company is expected to announce its results on Friday. We will see if Ambani, who has often said that the company’s biggest challenges are also its biggest opportunities, will be able to pull a rabbit from his hat.

##### Rising debt burden

![People on motorcycles and a bicycle pass in front of an HDFC Bank branch; a man stands outside talking on a phone.](https://images.ft.com/v3/image/raw/https%3A%2F%2Fd1e00ek4ebabms.cloudfront.net%2Fproduction%2Fd87cfe0f-97fb-4bfe-bedf-767bbaaa00ef.jpg?source=next-article&fit=scale-down&quality=highest&width=700&dpr=1)Indians are borrowing more to fund everyday expenses — and defaulting more © 2021 Bloomberg Finance LP
India’s prominent private banks announced their quarterly results over the past few days. A key takeaway from HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank and Yes Bank is the strong improvement in their lending businesses. ICICI Bank’s total loans rose 16 per cent annually, HDFC Bank’s grew 12 per cent and Yes Bank reported an 11 per cent increase. All three are seeing substantial momentum in retail lending, particularly in vehicle loans and personal debt.

While this is great news for banks, it raises concerns for the broader economy. I dug a little deeper.

Turns out, not only are [Indians borrowing more](https://www.ft.com/content/8f278011-bb0a-4ef3-a4da-8ce063f5bd64?syn-25a6b1a6=1), they are defaulting more too. Last month, the finance ministry informed the parliament that Indian banks wrote off loans worth Rs1.72tn in FY2024-25. Retail loans accounted for more than a quarter of these write-offs, and in fact, they were more than double that of the agricultural and industrial sectors.

Despite the economy growing at more than 7.5 per cent, many Indians appear unable to reduce their debt burden. More concerning is that mortgages do not constitute a significant portion of these loans. This suggests that borrowing is largely driven by consumption — covering both everyday expenses and discretionary spending such as holidays. Another major source of debt is purchases made on instalments, with many Indians falling into the trap of aspirational spending, often influenced by social media.

A report by Marcellus Investment Managers released last month highlighted that Indians were the most indebted consumers globally. Household debt (excluding mortgages) stands at 32.3 per cent of GDP — among the highest in the world, and according to the report, the gap between India and other major economies on this metric has widened significantly over the past four years.

The [double burden of stagnant wages and rising expenses](https://www.ft.com/content/c5a9c1a1-3eb0-47e9-9e78-2df4a85a4920) is trapping many Indians in a cycle of debt. Unfortunately, neither factor appears likely to ease in the near term. Inflation, which had remained relatively contained in recent quarters, is expected to rise again amid uncertainty around fuel and gas prices. These same pressures are likely to constrain wage growth across sectors. As a result, the debt burden that began during the pandemic shows little sign of abating.

##### Go figure

The US’s status as the world’s [lowest-cost dollar borrower is now facing a challenge](https://www.ft.com/content/ad217964-bcc9-468a-868f-810e118a4fbc?syn-25a6b1a6=1) as Trump risks pile up. This is driving up interest in what is called sovereign, supranational and agency (SSA) dollar debt. This week the European Investment Bank set a record after attracting more than $33bn of orders for a $4bn, three-year bond.
