## Highlights
- Some sources privately claim that Xi has lost real power and been sidelined. Others whisper that Xi’s health has deteriorated; the politician appearing in public, they say, is merely a lookalike, while a group of the party’s most venerable elder statesmen are actually calling the shots. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ka1whqv8gm8cwszgpmz1tbra))
- Such fanciful rumors are common in authoritarian systems, especially in the lead-up to major political events—such as the Chinese Communist Party’s fourth plenum, which took place in October, and whose outcomes are expected to set the country’s developmental direction for the next five years. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ka3ezyhh1fzkwkpxxvd0a5r0))
- The shared thread in all these rumors is the idea that a powerful group of party elders with access to insider information—leaders and high-level cadres who have usually stepped back from active political roles but remain influential behind the scenes—still hold enough clout to overrule Xi and shape China’s political course. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ka3f194qfr05dc3smqfh3b2b))
- Deng Xiaoping did to steer China away from radical politics in the late 1970s and to overrule more conservative leaders and insist on market-oriented economic reforms in the 1990s ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ka3f25hpekk1ghteapnjycz8))
- Xi has systematically dismantled the channels of influence through which elders previously operated, including the ability to appoint personnel and their ties within the military. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ka3f31hh43cytvdyp9w6xckm))
- In the early 1980s, to formalize their role, party leaders under Deng’s initiative established the Central Advisory Commission, a kind of upper chamber that gave elders an institutional platform to influence personnel and policy decisions ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ka3f65ztvwdxxx3dbv9ne4zr))
- As communist China’s revolutionary generation grew old and passed away, no new elders had the historical prestige or military leverage to inherit their authority. The party elders who succeeded them—the second and third generations—were mainly administrators whose authority derived from their former positions of power, not from revolutionary credentials ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ka3hc9sgcse2dyxfzk5085gr))
- But what fully ended elders’ power was Xi’s systematic attack on personal networks. Xi’s anticorruption campaign, which he launched soon after taking the reins of the party, in 2012, cut the informal bonds that once linked current and retired elites. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ka3hejftsnc0s8fzdhwseza6))
- He methodically dismantled alternate power centers in the security services, the military, and strategically important sectors such as energy and finance. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ka3heqd8f1ep7fgqvfyj8fab))
- At the same time, Xi increased oversight of retired senior cadres and introduced new rules prohibiting them from publicly commenting on the country’s leaders or their policies. Xi even reclassified dinners among elders as potential matters of political security, which meant that they could no longer visit one another freely. If elders wished to influence Xi, their avenues of influence dried up. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ka3hf5dkkehx6z2y19j0091h))
- With Xi’s consolidation of control and the natural evolution of China’s political system, elders no longer have the capacity to mobilize a coalition that fuses personnel leverage, military clout, procedural know-how, and moral authority in a way that could seriously check the current leadership. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ka3hgr9tqy7a89y9hesheefq))
- A more institutionalized bureaucracy can slow decision-making and resist the personal whims of individual leaders by subjecting them to lengthy formal processes ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ka3hmdt641vw3dwmw4c7f0e2))
- China’s troubled response to the [COVID-19](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/tags/coronavirus) pandemic reveals the dangers of this new situation. A group of powerful elders could not have prevented the outbreak. But it might have limited the damage by making it easier for information to flow to decision-makers. Elders could have pushed leaders to heed early warning signs when the virus first started spreading, in 2020, and forced Xi to relax his draconian policies sooner as the pandemic dragged on into its second and third years. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ka3hs0gmpg4re5bw2a3g7kkj))
- Of the partial substitutes that have emerged to replace the ballast once provided by party elders, only external pressure—chiefly the impacts of U.S.-Chinese competition and confrontation—has significantly limited Xi’s power. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01ka3hscd1b9td0d1x8tv5bxwp))
