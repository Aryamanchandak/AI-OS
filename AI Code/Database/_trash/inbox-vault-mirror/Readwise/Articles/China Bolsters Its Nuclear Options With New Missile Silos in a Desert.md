## Highlights
- Researchers in the United States have identified the construction of 119 new intercontinental ballistic missile silos in a desert in northwestern [China](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/11/03/us/politics/china-military-nuclear.html), indicating that the country is carrying out plans to strengthen its strategic nuclear capability. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k8sz379zj67d4a1pf5mr8m9g))
- China’s most recent defense strategy, released in 2019, said it would keep “its nuclear capabilities at the minimum level required for national security.” It has also vowed not to use nuclear weapons first or against any non-nuclear state ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k8sz512mzdmm0f90k7wjsf3e))
- The silo construction is not unexpected, though the speed and the scope of it surprised the researchers who studied them ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k8sz59pda0hahz5wx23zes31))
- The Pentagon’s [latest report](https://media.defense.gov/2020/Sep/01/2002488689/-1/-1/1/2020-DOD-CHINA-MILITARY-POWER-REPORT-FINAL.PDF) on China’s military forces, released last fall, estimated that the Chinese maintain “an operational nuclear warhead stockpile in the low-200’s,” including about 100 intercontinental ballistic missiles. The report said that China intended to strengthen its “nuclear triad” of strategic weapons that would allow it to launch nuclear weapons from land, sea and air. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k8sz5vdvn54kr9s80761gxw8))
- The Yumen site’s design does not necessarily mean China intends to deploy another 100 missiles there. Instead, it could reflect a strategy the United States considered in the 1970s in which a fewer number of missiles are moved among a larger network of silos like a “shell game,” making it harder for an adversary to destroy them in a first strike. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k8t3zj6ahq8gf0p8wnz5j040))
