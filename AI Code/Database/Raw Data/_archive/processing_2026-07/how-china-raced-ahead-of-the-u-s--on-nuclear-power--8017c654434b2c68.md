## Highlights
- In 2013, construction began on the first two new U.S. nuclear reactors in a generation. Atomic energy was back. Or was it?
  Seven years late and $17 billion over budget, the reactors became two of the costliest ever built. Once again, nuclear power seemed hopeless, at least in the U.S.
  Yet over the same period, China built 13 similar reactors, with 33 more underway. And Beijing’s nuclear ambitions are global. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k87rj3jjrnhhnmv4ayhdt2p1))
- China is quickly becoming the global leader in nuclear power, with [nearly as many reactors under construction](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/current-and-future-generation/plans-for-new-reactors-worldwide) as the rest of the world combined ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k87rja9j4s9bp668a9yn55m8))
- By 2030, China’s nuclear capacity is set to surpass that of the United States, the first country to split atoms to make electricity. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k87rjjcqsmvpnk3v3wnwy807))
- At the same time, China is pushing the envelope, making [breakthroughs in next-generation nuclear technologies](https://itif.org/publications/2024/06/17/how-innovative-is-china-in-nuclear-power/) that have eluded the West. The country is also [investing heavily in fusion](https://ucigcc.org/blog/fusion-and-chinas-quest-for-energy-independence/), a potentially limitless source of clean power if anyone can figure out how to tame it ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k87rjvkna5nddq1rndeb1x74))
- Beijing’s ultimate objective is to become a supplier of nuclear power to the world, joining the rare few nations — including the United States, Russia, France and South Korea — that can design and export some of the most sophisticated machines ever invented. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k87rkxp5gk0nzbesbge0wd6e))
- The Trump administration wants to [quadruple U.S. nuclear power capacity](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/05/23/climate/trump-executive-order-nuclear-power.html) by 2050, even as it ignores global warming, and it hopes to develop a new generation of reactor technology to power data centers at home and sell to energy-hungry countries overseas. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k87rqaazctzfrcfp3rkgvmm6))
- Yet in the race for atomic energy, China has one clear advantage: It has figured out how to produce reactors relatively quickly and cheaply. The country now assembles reactors in just five to six years, twice as fast as Western nations. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k87rqspr734myr2yp8vysv18))
- While U.S. nuclear construction costs skyrocketed after the 1960s, they fell by half in China during the 2000s and have since stabilized ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k87rr3dn6vmhkd3v0jkza8kd))
- Financing these multibillion-dollar projects is staggeringly difficult. Even minor problems, like needing regulator approval to modify a component midway through, can lead to long delays and can cause borrowing costs to skyrocket. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k87rvprczera31e0096kwaay))
- It starts with heavy government support. Three state-owned nuclear developers receive cheap government-backed loans to build new reactors, which is valuable since financing can be one-third of costs. The Chinese government also requires electric grid operators to buy some of the power from nuclear plants at favorable rates. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k87rwbhp7jstetk59t20mvkf))
- Just as importantly, China’s nuclear companies build only a handful of reactor types and they do it over and over again. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k87rwj7jnyjwvmwrfnvewd0g))
- The fact that the Chinese government has a national mandate to expand nuclear power means that companies can confidently invest in domestic factories and a dedicated engineering work force ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k87rx50gnsndt54yh6yvdd2y))
- U.S. nuclear power died from a lack of predictability. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k87s0yfq5d4eywf9arjrdv5k))
- The contrast became glaring in the late 2000s, when U.S. utilities tried to revive nuclear power with a new reactor model called the AP1000, with improved safety features. Developers [struggled with the novel technology](https://www.nytimes.com/2017/02/18/business/energy-environment/nuclear-power-westinghouse-toshiba.html), leading to repeated delays and soaring costs. By the time the two reactors in Georgia were finished last year, most utilities were hesitant to try again. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k87s1pajyazh6ytm019dg8tz))
- As it happened, China built AP1000s at the same time. It, too, faced severe challenges, such as difficulties in obtaining coolant pumps and unpredictable cost spikes. But instead of giving up, Chinese officials [studied what went wrong](https://sai.inl.gov/content/uploads/29/2025/06/M3_SAI-AP1000_Lessons_Rev6-nocomments-002.pdf) and concluded they needed to tweak the design and develop domestic supply chains. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88k3h6jksxbspbtgqrkdv8p))
- “What the Chinese did was really smart,” said James Krellenstein, the chief executive of Alva Energy, a nuclear consultancy. “They said, we’re going to pause for a few years and incorporate every lesson learned.” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88k3se0fxfme9q82aqzxez9))
- China is now building nine more copies of that reactor, known as the CAP1000, all on pace to be completed [within five years at a drastically lower cost](https://sai.inl.gov/content/uploads/29/2025/06/M3_SAI-AP1000_Lessons_Rev6-nocomments-002.pdf) ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88k3z063c45zn75jf451mcn))
- Nuclear proponents in the United States sometimes argue that overly strict safety regulations drive up costs. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88k4bnzmapgvbs0qkngsxbn))
- China’s safety requirements are similar. But in China [the approval process is more predictable](https://thebreakthrough.org/issues/nuclear-energy-innovation/what-can-the-u-s-learn-from-chinese-nuclear-deployment), and opponents have fewer ways to challenge a project ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88k4gkh4vx53hpk4rtgwtj4))
- Most reactors in China break ground weeks after receiving final approval from the safety regulator, ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88k4yz7n3k9dk46hrwrss86))
- In the United States, by contrast, projects often need additional permits from state governments that can take months or years ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88k533364jh5jaqcq7cwkvw))
- “China is practiced at building really big things, everything from dams to highways to high speed rail, and those project management skills are transferable,” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88k5c1294pyz19hpqxgwtvf))
- More nuclear power could help backstop renewables and displace coal. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88k7c8mazxc5f88s82zhn7p))
- China’s nuclear expansion still faces hurdles. One of China’s plants [suffered a smaller radioactive leak](https://www.cnn.com/2021/06/14/politics/china-nuclear-reactor-leak-us-monitoring) in 2021, and a bigger accident could trigger a public backlash ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88k978zfwkbsxj4w38me1xc))
- The country is still figuring out where to bury its nuclear waste, and some cities [have seen impassioned protests](https://www.nytimes.com/2016/08/11/world/asia/china-nuclear-fuel-lianyungang.html) over plans for waste reprocessing plants ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88k9y8kbkgh4nbwa8nev6kp))
- Beijing has also [blocked new reactors](https://www.oxfordenergy.org/wpcms/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Nuclear-Power-In-China-CE3.pdf) in much of China’s interior over concerns about their water use. If that moratorium persists, it could limit the industry’s growth. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88kahjpv0m6xrx0bcavwsq0))
- For now, though, the country is barreling ahead, with plans to build hundreds of reactors by midcentury. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88kb0vfmk0gcm4na2w81ehs))
- In the United States, nuclear power is one of the rare types of energy that has support from Republican and Democratic politicians alike, especially as demand for electricity rises. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88kbejp8ynxrbghry4jyb0y))
- Yet the U.S. is pursuing a starkly different path to nuclear expansion, one that leans more heavily on private innovation than government backing. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88kbpkjac12dmdytjg86wp4))
- Dozens of start-ups are working on [a new generation of smaller reactors](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2023/11/12/climate/nuclear-reactors-clean-energy.html) meant to be cheaper than the hulking plants of old ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88kbwh7cr1aadnezahavs67))
- Tech companies like Google, Amazon and OpenAI are pouring billions into nuclear start-ups like Kairos Power, X-Energy and Oklo to help power their data centers for artificial intelligence ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88kc47t9fdjswry9zj5n1x0))
- Yet some worry that the United States is betting too heavily on technological breakthroughs instead of focusing on the financing, skills and infrastructure needed to build plants, as China has. The U.S., for instance, [has lost almost all of its heavy forging capacity](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/nuclear-fuel-cycle/nuclear-power-reactors/heavy-manufacturing-of-power-plants) to make large reactor components. A new generation of advanced reactors could also take years to perfect, leaving America behind. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88kdhaxfdya71nt7t9pm8dz))
- While the Trump administration has moved to [speed up nuclear permitting](https://www.energy.gov/articles/energy-department-announces-new-pathway-test-advanced-reactors) and [increase domestic supplies of nuclear fuel](https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/energy-department-establish-new-consortium-nuclear-fuel-supply-chain), some important government tools for advancing new reactors, such as the Energy Department’s loan office, have been [hampered by staffing cuts](https://efifoundation.org/foundation-reports/analysis/doe-staff-crunch-slows-american-energy-innovation/). Efforts to slash safety regulations could be contentious. There is also a risk that interest by tech giants could fizzle if the A.I. boom slows. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88kfpa9g2989h6swt37xhdd))
- China’s fast-paced nuclear program is a prelude to a larger goal: dominating the global market. Chinese companies [have already built](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/country-profiles/countries-o-s/pakistan) six reactors in Pakistan and plan to export many more. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88kgxqw9cer2tmr3n4vs7me))
- At the same time, China is working to surpass the United States in technological innovation. China has built what it calls the world’s first “fourth generation” reactor, [a gas-cooled model](https://www.world-nuclear-news.org/Articles/Chinese-HTR-PM-Demo-begins-commercial-operation) that can provide heat and steam for heavy industry in addition to electricity ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88khbrj4scmp7qhs95pxm7y))
- The Chinese are also pursuing technologies that use less uranium, such as [thorium reactors](https://www.neimagazine.com/news/china-refuels-thorium-reactor-without-shutdown/), or [recycle spent nuclear fuel](https://spectrum.ieee.org/china-breeder-reactor). It’s a recognition that China doesn’t have enough domestic uranium for a massive build-out of traditional reactors. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88khwjnc4zf28ersjr9ng3x))
- Even if U.S. companies and labs remain at the forefront of innovation, one recent report warned that [China was 10 to 15 years ahead](https://itif.org/publications/2024/06/17/how-innovative-is-china-in-nuclear-power/) of the United States in its ability to deploy next-generation reactors widely. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88kj7fmeyq3n1ayart4y3a4))
- It’s a familiar story: The United States invented solar panels and batteries, only to watch as China scaled those technologies and now controls global markets. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88kjce921hrykbptw5ptnqa))
- “Maybe we can convince some of our allies not to buy Chinese reactors, but there are going to be plenty of other countries out there with growing energy demands,” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88kjkfx7y9k8fkp8mvf32py))
- “And if America isn’t ready, we won’t be able to compete.” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01k88kjq704d1k8hja8wke4z20))
